China Mortgage LPR and Repricing: Why Your Payment Changes Late

Most Chinese mortgages are priced off the Loan Prime Rate and re-price once a year. Two dates decide what you pay: the LPR announcement and your own re-pricing date. Understanding how they interact explains why your payment changes in a different month than the news.

1. What the LPR is

The Loan Prime Rate is published monthly by the National Interbank Funding Center under authorisation of the People's Bank of China. Two tenors matter: the one-year LPR, which normally anchors short-term business lending, and the over-five-year LPR, which anchors mortgage pricing.

As of the October 2026 publication, the one-year LPR is 3.0% and the over-five-year LPR is 3.5%. The same rates have been in place for a sustained period, which means many borrowers have not seen their payments move despite frequent headlines.

2. Your rate is LPR plus a spread

A mortgage rate is expressed as the reference rate plus or minus a fixed number of basis points agreed at origination. The spread never changes for the life of the loan; only the reference moves.

Your rate = over-five-year LPR + your fixed spread

This is why two neighbours who bought in different years see their rates move together but remain different in level.

3. The re-pricing date

The rate is not updated the moment the LPR changes. It is re-set on a re-pricing date specified in your contract, and the reference used is the LPR in force on that date. Two conventions are common:

  • 1 January each year — using the LPR published in the December of the previous year.
  • The anniversary of loan disbursement — using the LPR in force on that date.

The payment actually changes from the period following re-pricing, so a loan that re-prices on 1 January shows the new instalment in the first payment of the new year.

An LPR cut in June does not lower your payment in June. If your re-pricing date is 1 January, you wait until the following January — and if the LPR has risen again by December, you get none of the benefit.

4. Why the first payment after re-pricing looks odd

Re-pricing recalculates the split between principal and interest on the outstanding balance. The instalment changes, but the first payment after re-pricing can look unusual because the interest for the partial period is computed at the old rate and the new rate in different segments. This is a calculation artefact, not an error.

5. Existing loans converted from the old benchmark

Loans written against the previous benchmark rate were converted to an LPR basis with a spread chosen so that the rate at conversion matched the old rate exactly. From that point on, the loan moves with the LPR. Borrowers who chose a fixed rate instead keep that rate for the remaining term.

6. Housing provident fund loans are different

Provident fund loans do not follow the LPR. Their rates are set directly by the central bank. Following the reduction effective from 8 May 2025, the rate on a first-home fund loan of more than five years is 2.6%, and on a second-home fund loan of more than five years, 3.075%.

For loans already outstanding before that date, the adjusted rate took effect from 1 January 2026, applied automatically with no application required.

On a combo loan, only the fund portion follows the fund rate. The commercial portion follows the LPR. The two portions re-price on different schedules and at different rates.

7. What to check on your own loan

  • Your re-pricing date — 1 January or the disbursement anniversary.
  • Your spread — stated in basis points, positive or negative, fixed for the term.
  • Which reference applies — over-five-year LPR for commercial, central bank rate for the fund portion.
  • Whether you chose fixed — if so, the LPR is irrelevant to you.

8. Should you act on an expected change

Trying to time prepayment around LPR announcements rarely helps, because the reference rate you will get is the one in force on your re-pricing date, not the one reported today. What does help is knowing your date, so you can plan a prepayment for a point when the balance is still large and before re-pricing resets the schedule.

9. Where the reference rate stands now

The over-five-year LPR published on 20 October 2026 is 3.5%, unchanged for seventeen consecutive months. The one-year LPR is 3.0%.

Commercial housing loans are priced off the over-five-year tenor. Because the reference has been stable for so long, most borrowers with a 1 January repricing date have seen the same rate applied for several consecutive years.

Provident fund loans do not follow the LPR at all. Under the central bank's reduction effective 8 May 2025, the rate above five years is 2.6% for a first home and 3.075% for a second home; for terms of five years or less it is 2.1% and 2.525% respectively. Existing fund loans moved to these rates on 1 January 2026.

10. The two repricing conventions in practice

ConventionReference usedWhen the payment changes
1 January each yearThe LPR published in the preceding DecemberFrom the first payment of the new year
Anniversary of disbursementThe LPR in force on that dateFrom the payment following the anniversary

The gap between a change in the reference and a change in your payment can therefore be up to a year. A cut in June does nothing for a January repricer until the following January — and if the reference has risen again by December, none of the benefit arrives at all.

11. What a rate change does to the payment

On ¥1,000,000 over 30 years, if the reference moves from 3.5% to 3.0%:

  • Payment before: ¥4,490.45
  • Payment after: ¥4,216.04
  • Saving: ¥274.41 a month, about ¥3,293 a year

For a loan already part-way through its term the saving is smaller, because less interest remains. On the same loan after 60 payments, the balance is ¥896,971; repricing that to 3.0% over the remaining 300 months gives ¥4,253.54 — a saving of ¥236.91 against the previous ¥4,490.45.

12. Why the first payment after repricing looks odd

Repricing changes the split between principal and interest on the outstanding balance, but it does not restart the loan. The instalment is recalculated on the remaining balance over the remaining term. Where the change date falls inside a payment period, interest for the two segments is computed at the old and new rates separately, so the first instalment after repricing can differ slightly from the steady-state figure that follows.

This is a calculation artefact, not an error. If the second payment after repricing does not match your own calculation, check whether the first one contained a split-period adjustment.

13. Fixed or floating: how to think about it

  • Floating reprices automatically. You collect cuts without applying for anything, and you absorb rises automatically too.
  • A fixed rate is a one-way choice. It protects you if rates rise and costs you if they fall, and it usually cannot be reversed without refinancing.
  • Compare against the break-even. Estimate how large a rise you would need to see before the fixed rate wins, then judge how likely that is over your expected holding period.

With the reference unchanged for seventeen months, the practical difference has been small. That is a description of the present, not a forecast.

14. What to check on your own loan

  • What is my current rate, and what is my fixed spread over the reference?
  • What is my repricing date — 1 January, or the disbursement anniversary?
  • Is my loan a floating LPR loan, a fixed-rate loan, or a provident fund loan? The three behave differently.
  • If I have a combo loan, do the two portions reprice on the same date?
  • Does any current interest-subsidy policy apply to my loan, and does it change at repricing?

These five answers explain every change you will see in your payment over the life of the loan.
