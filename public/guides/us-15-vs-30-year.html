15-Year vs 30-Year Mortgage: The Real Trade-Off

The 30-year mortgage is the default almost everywhere in the US, but the 15-year is not simply "the same loan, faster". Choosing between them changes the payment, the total interest, and how much financial flexibility you retain. This is the arithmetic behind that choice.

1. The two loans side by side

$400,000 at 6.5%, comparing a 30-year term with a 15-year term:

30-year15-year
Monthly principal and interest$2,528.27$3,484.43
Total interest over the term$510,178$227,197
Monthly difference$956.16
Interest saved$282,981
The 15-year saves about $283,000 in interest and costs about $956 more every month. Both numbers are large, and which one dominates depends on what you do with the difference.

2. Why the interest gap is so wide

Two effects compound. First, you borrow for half as long, so interest accrues for half the time. Second, a much larger share of every payment goes to principal from the first month, so the balance falls faster. Neither is a trick — it is just amortisation over a shorter schedule.

3. The rate is usually lower too

15-year loans typically price below 30-year loans, often by half a percentage point or more. The comparison above holds the rate constant at 6.5% to isolate the effect of the term alone; in practice the 15-year would likely be cheaper still.

4. What the extra $956 could do instead

Taking the 30-year and investing the difference is the standard counter-argument. It is valid, but it depends on three assumptions:

  • You actually invest the difference — every month, for years. Most people do not, and the money is absorbed into general spending.
  • Your after-tax return exceeds the mortgage rate — the mortgage saving is risk-free and tax-free in effect; market returns are neither.
  • You keep the loan long enough — the interest saving accrues over decades.
Comparing a guaranteed 6.5% saving against an uncertain market return is not an equal-risk comparison. That does not make the 30-year wrong — it means the decision is about risk tolerance and discipline, not just arithmetic.

5. The flexibility argument

A 30-year payment is an obligation floor, not a target. You can always pay more. A 15-year payment is a commitment: if income drops, you cannot reduce it without refinancing, and refinancing is not guaranteed to be available or cheap.

This asymmetry is why the 30-year is often the better choice for variable income, early career, or anyone without a large emergency reserve.

6. The middle option

You do not have to choose only these two. Alternatives include:

  • A 20-year term — a genuine compromise, though less commonly quoted.
  • Take the 30-year and prepay deliberately — an extra payment amount applied to principal each month shortens the term without committing you to it.
  • One extra payment per year — splitting your monthly payment into half-payments every two weeks produces 26 half-payments, i.e. 13 monthly payments a year, and cuts years off the schedule.

7. When the 15-year is clearly better

  • You are late in your career with stable, predictable income.
  • You have a fully funded emergency reserve and are already maxing retirement contributions.
  • You are risk-averse and value a paid-off home over market exposure.
  • You are refinancing later in the original term, when the payment jump is smaller.

8. When the 30-year is clearly better

  • Your income is variable or commission-based.
  • You are early in your career and expect income to grow.
  • You need the cash flow for other high-priority goals.
  • You can reliably earn more on capital than the mortgage costs — and you will actually deploy it.

9. How to decide with your own numbers

Price both terms at the rates you are actually quoted, then look at three figures: the monthly difference, the total interest difference, and whether the higher payment still fits inside your budget after tax, insurance and savings. If the higher payment forces you to cut retirement contributions or emergency savings, the 30-year wins regardless of the interest saved.

10. What happens when the 15-year also gets a lower rate

The comparison above holds the rate constant to isolate the effect of the term. In practice, 15-year loans usually price below 30-year loans — often by half a percentage point or more. Adding that to the same $400,000:

30-year at 6.5%15-year at 6.5%15-year at 5.5%
Monthly payment$2,528.27$3,484.43$3,268.33
Total interest$510,178$227,197$188,300
Interest versus 30-year—−$282,981−$321,878
Extra monthly cost—+$956.16+$740.06

The lower rate does two things at once: it narrows the monthly gap from $956.16 to $740.06 and widens the interest saving from $282,981 to $321,878. Both effects move in the borrower's favour, which is why the real-world case for the 15-year is stronger than the constant-rate comparison suggests.

11. The third option: take the 30-year and pay it like a 15

There is a structure that captures most of the interest saving while keeping the flexibility of the lower payment. Borrow on 30 years, then pay the 15-year amount voluntarily.

  • Paying $956.16 extra each month on the 30-year loan clears the balance in 180 months — exactly 15 years.
  • Total interest lands essentially where the 15-year loan puts it, because the balance follows the same path.
  • The difference: if income drops, you can stop paying the extra. The obligation stays at $2,528.27, not $3,484.43.

The cost of this flexibility is discipline. It only works if the extra payment is actually made, every month, without exception. That is a behavioural requirement, not a financial one — and it is where most people fail.

12. How the choice interacts with investing

The standard counter-argument is to take the 30-year and invest the difference. Whether it wins depends on three conditions, and all three must hold:

  • The difference is actually invested — every month, for the full term. Money absorbed into general spending earns nothing.
  • The after-tax return beats the mortgage rate. A 6.5% mortgage saving is risk-free; a market return is neither risk-free nor guaranteed, and it may be taxed.
  • The horizon is long enough. The interest saving compounds over decades; a short holding period favours neither structure clearly.

Comparing a guaranteed saving against a risky return at the same percentage is not an equal-risk comparison. Adjusted for risk, the mortgage saving is worth more than its face value.

13. Which loan fits which borrower

SituationBetter fitReason
Variable or commission income30-yearThe payment is a floor, not a commitment
Stable high income, near peak earning years15-yearFront-loads repayment while income is highest
Approaching retirement15-year, or shorterClears the debt before income falls
Early career, income expected to rise30-yearKeeps early payments low
Strong investment discipline30-yearThe difference can be deployed elsewhere
No emergency reserve yet30-yearFlexibility is worth more than the saving
Likely to move within five yearsEitherThe interest gap barely materialises

When the numbers are close, choose the payment you can still make in a bad year. The interest difference is recoverable; a missed payment is not.
