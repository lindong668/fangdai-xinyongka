Debt-to-Income Ratio: How Much House You Can Actually Afford

Lenders do not approve you for the payment you can manage — they approve you for the payment that fits inside two ratios. Understanding those ratios tells you your real price ceiling before you start viewing homes.

1. The two ratios

Debt-to-income compares what you owe each month against what you earn each month. Mortgage lending uses two versions:

  • Front-end ratio — housing payment only (PITI plus HOA) divided by gross monthly income. The conventional guideline is 28%.
  • Back-end ratio — housing payment plus all other recurring debt (car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, child support) divided by gross monthly income. The guideline is 36%.

This is the origin of the "28/36 rule". At $8,000 of gross monthly income:

RatioLimitWhat it covers
28% front-end$2,240Housing only
36% back-end$2,880Housing plus all other debt

2. Gross, not net

Both ratios use gross monthly income — before tax. This is the most common source of surprise. If your take-home pay is $6,000 and your gross is $8,000, the lender computes against $8,000, which makes the affordable payment look larger than your budget actually supports.

The lender's ceiling and your comfort level are different numbers. Qualifying for $2,880 does not mean $2,880 will feel comfortable alongside childcare, insurance, commuting and savings.

3. What counts as debt

Counted: auto loans and leases, student loans (even deferred ones, usually at a stated payment), credit card minimum payments, personal loans, alimony and child support, and any co-signed obligation.

Generally not counted: groceries, utilities, insurance premiums, 401(k) contributions, and balances you pay in full each month.

4. Why the front-end ratio binds first

If you carry no other debt, the back-end ratio is irrelevant and the front-end 28% governs. Most buyers who get declined on ratio grounds are not declined because of the housing payment — they are declined because existing obligations consume the back-end allowance before housing is added.

This is why paying off a car loan before applying can raise your approval amount substantially, while the same money applied to the down payment might not.

5. How loan type changes the limit

  • Conventional loans — the 28/36 guideline, with some flexibility for strong credit or large reserves.
  • FHA loans — more tolerant, often around 31% front-end and 43% back-end, and in some cases higher with compensating factors.
  • VA loans — no fixed DTI cap; uses a residual income test instead.

6. What is not in the ratio

DTI says nothing about cash. Two buyers with identical ratios can have very different outcomes, because the calculation ignores:

  • The down payment — a larger one reduces the loan and therefore the payment, which improves both ratios.
  • Cash reserves — months of payments held after closing, required by many programs.
  • Credit score — it drives the rate, and the rate drives the payment.

7. Working backwards from your income

Rather than starting from a home price, start from the payment:

  1. Take your gross monthly income.
  2. Multiply by 0.28 for the housing ceiling, and by 0.36 for the total debt ceiling.
  3. Subtract your existing monthly debt payments from the back-end figure.
  4. Whichever of the two results is smaller is your real housing budget — and it includes tax, insurance and PMI, not just principal and interest.
The payment in step 4 is PITI, not the loan payment. Property tax, insurance and mortgage insurance can add several hundred dollars, which corresponds to a materially smaller loan than buyers expect.

8. How to improve your number

  • Retire small debts before applying. Eliminating a $300 monthly car payment can free more approval room than a modest income increase.
  • Avoid new credit. Financing furniture or a car during the process can undo months of preparation.
  • Add a co-borrower whose income counts and whose debt is low.
  • Increase the down payment to shrink the loan.

9. Working backwards from income to a purchase price

The ratio gives you a payment ceiling; amortisation turns that into a loan, and the down payment turns the loan into a price. On $8,000 of gross monthly income, at 6.5% over 30 years, with the same $400 tax and $116.67 insurance:

  • Front-end limit at 28%: $2,240 a month
  • Less tax and insurance: $1,723.33 available for principal and interest
  • Supported loan: about $272,649
  • At 20% down, supported price: about $340,812
Tax and insurance consume about 23% of the housing allowance before the loan is even considered. Any estimate that converts income directly into a loan size without deducting them is optimistic by roughly a quarter.

10. How existing debt shrinks your approval

The back-end ratio is what most borrowers fail. Every recurring obligation spends part of the 36% allowance before housing is added. With $8,000 of income:

Existing monthly debtBack-end allowanceLeft for housing
$0$2,880$2,880
$500 (a car loan)$2,880$2,380
$1,000 (car plus student loans)$2,880$1,880

A $500 car payment costs $500 of housing capacity — dollar for dollar. Retiring it before applying is often worth more than saving the same $500 toward the down payment, because it raises the ceiling rather than reducing the loan.

11. Student loans and deferred balances

How deferred or income-driven student loans are counted depends on the loan program, and the treatment has changed more than once. In broad terms:

  • Lenders generally impute a monthly obligation for deferred loans rather than counting zero.
  • Income-driven repayment payments are usually counted at the actual payment shown.
  • Conventional, FHA and VA programs apply different conventions.

Ask your lender how your specific balances will be counted before you apply. The difference can move the approval amount by tens of thousands of dollars.

12. What sits outside the ratio

The ratio does not measure whether the payment is comfortable. It measures whether it fits a guideline. Costs that never appear in it include childcare, commuting, healthcare, insurance, saving for retirement, and maintenance — typically 1% or more of the property value each year.

A payment that passes at 36% can still be unaffordable. Treat the ratio as a ceiling set by the lender, not as a budget set by you.

13. Improving your number before you apply

  • Retire small debts. Every dollar of monthly obligation removed is a dollar added to the housing allowance.
  • Avoid new obligations. Financing a car or furniture before closing can undo months of preparation.
  • Increase the down payment. It lowers the loan, and at 20% it removes mortgage insurance entirely.
  • Add a co-borrower carefully. Their income helps only if their debts do not consume more than they add.
  • Check your credit report first. A higher score lowers both the rate and the insurance premium.

DTI is the most fixable of all the approval criteria, because unlike your income it responds directly to decisions you make in the months before applying.
