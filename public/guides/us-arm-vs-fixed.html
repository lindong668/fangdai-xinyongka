ARM vs Fixed-Rate Mortgage: How Adjustable Rates Reset and When They Pay Off

An adjustable-rate mortgage offers a genuinely lower payment for its fixed period, then reprices against a market index. Whether that trade is worth taking depends on one thing nobody can promise you: what rates do next. This guide explains the reset mechanics, the caps that limit the damage, and runs the numbers on a 5/1 ARM against a 30-year fixed.

1. What an ARM actually is

An ARM has a fixed introductory period followed by scheduled adjustments. The label tells you the shape: a 5/1 ARM is fixed for five years, then adjusts every year. A 7/1 fixes for seven; a 10/1 for ten.

After the fixed period, the rate is rebuilt from two parts:

  • Index — a published benchmark the loan tracks
  • Margin — a fixed percentage the lender adds, set at closing and never changing

Index plus margin equals your new rate. When the index rises, so does your payment; when it falls, your payment can fall too, subject to the caps.

2. The caps that protect you

Every ARM carries three limits, usually written as a sequence:

  • Initial adjustment cap — how far the rate can jump at the first reset
  • Periodic cap — the maximum change at each subsequent adjustment
  • Lifetime cap — the ceiling the rate can never exceed over the whole loan

Read these before signing. Two ARMs with identical start rates can behave completely differently once the fixed period ends.

3. The fixed-rate baseline

On a $400,000 purchase with 20% down, the loan is $320,000. At 6.5% fixed over 30 years, principal and interest is $2,022.62 — unchanged for three hundred and sixty months.

4. The ARM, years one to five

Same loan at a 5.5% introductory rate:

  • Initial payment: $1,816.92
  • Monthly saving versus fixed: $205.69
  • Cumulative saving over 60 months: $12,342

That is real money, and it arrives in the years when moving costs, furnishings and repairs tend to be heaviest.

5. The reset

After five years the balance stands at about $295,874. If the rate resets to 7% with 25 years remaining, the payment becomes $2,091.18.

That is $68.56 more per month than the fixed loan you declined. The saving you banked in the first five years — $12,342 — covers about 180 months of that gap. Past that point, the ARM is the more expensive choice.

6. When an ARM makes sense

  • You expect to sell or refinance inside the fixed window. You collect the discount and leave before any reset. This is the cleanest case.
  • Your income will rise predictably. A later, higher payment is easier to absorb.
  • The discount is genuinely large. A small introductory gap does not compensate for carrying reset risk.

7. When it does not

  • This is your long-term home. You will eventually live through several resets.
  • Your budget has no slack. If a higher payment would be genuinely painful, the risk is not worth a few hundred dollars.
  • You have not stress-tested the ceiling. Compute the payment at the lifetime cap. If you could not make that payment, the loan is not suitable.

8. How to decide

Run three scenarios: the rate stays flat, the rate hits the initial cap at first reset, and the rate reaches the lifetime cap. Compare the ARM payment in each against the fixed payment you gave up. If the ARM only wins in the first scenario, you are taking a risk whose downside you have already priced — and declined to pay for.

9. What the index actually is

Most ARMs written today track a published benchmark rather than a rate the lender invents. Understanding which benchmark matters, because it determines how your payment behaves when markets move.

  • SOFR-based indices — the standard replacement for the old LIBOR benchmarks, published daily and reflecting short-term funding markets.
  • The margin — a fixed percentage set at closing, commonly around 2 to 3 percentage points, that never changes.
  • The rounding — the resulting rate is usually rounded to the nearest one-eighth of a percentage point.

Ask which index, which margin, and the rounding convention. Two ARMs advertising the same 5.5% start rate can reprice very differently if one tracks a faster-moving index or carries a wider margin.

10. What the reset looks like at three different rates

On the example — a $320,000 loan, $295,874 outstanding after five years, 25 years remaining — the reset payment depends entirely on where the rate lands:

The $12,342 banked during the fixed period covers the 7% gap for about 180 months — fifteen years. Against an 8% reset it covers roughly 47 months. The spread of outcomes is wide, and it is not in your control.

11. What happens if rates fall instead

ARMs adjust in both directions, subject to the periodic cap. Most reduce the payment at the next adjustment rather than mid-period, so a fall in the index does not immediately lower your bill. Some loans permit a one-time conversion to a fixed rate for a fee — worth asking about, because it gives you an exit that does not require a full refinance.

12. The break-even horizon

The honest way to judge an ARM is to ask how long you must stay for the initial saving to survive the reset. The arithmetic:

  • Total saving during the fixed period: $12,342 over 60 months.
  • Monthly penalty after resetting to 7%: $68.56.
  • Months until the saving is exhausted: $12,342 ÷ $68.56 ≈ 180.

If you expect to sell or refinance within fifteen years of the reset, the ARM wins on these figures. If you expect to hold the loan to term, the fixed loan does. Everything else is commentary.

13. Questions to ask before signing

  • Which index and what margin? What is the rounding convention?
  • What are the three caps — initial, periodic and lifetime? Write them down.
  • Is there a prepayment penalty, and in which period does it apply?
  • Is the interest-only option present, and can the payment jump when the interest-only period ends?
  • Can the loan be converted to a fixed rate later, and at what cost?
  • What is the worst-case payment at the lifetime cap, and can I still afford it?

That last question is the one that matters. If the answer is no, the ARM is not suitable regardless of how attractive the start rate looks.
