Sending Money Abroad: How to Compare the True Cost

Two providers can quote the same exchange rate and differ by hundreds in what actually arrives. The rate is only one of several charges, and the rest are structured differently by every provider. This guide gives you a single method to compare any two transfers honestly, shows where the costs hide, and explains why small transfers are disproportionately expensive.

1. The only number that matters

Headline rates, "zero fee" banners and promotional margins are marketing. The comparable figure is one thing:

The amount the recipient actually receives, in their currency, after every charge has been deducted.

Get this from each provider before you compare. If a provider cannot or will not tell you, that is itself the answer. A quote that stops at "our rate is X" has not told you the price.

2. Compute the effective cost in one line

Four steps:

  1. Look up the mid-market rate for your currency pair at that moment.
  2. Multiply your send amount by it — that is the "perfect" outcome, with no costs at all.
  3. Subtract the amount the recipient will actually receive.
  4. Divide by the "perfect" outcome and express it as a percentage.
Effective cost % = (mid-market result − amount received) ÷ mid-market result × 100

A full example

You send 5,000. The mid-market rate to the destination currency is 7.20, so the perfect outcome is 36,000.

Note what this table reveals. Service B advertises "no fee" and the best rate, and on these numbers it does deliver the most — but compare it with the airport bureau, which advertises zero commission and is more than seventeen times as expensive. Neither headline tells you that; only the received amount does. Only the final received amount exposes either fact.

On a 5,000 transfer, the gap between the best and worst option here is about 1,650 — money that disappears entirely into spreads and fees.

3. The four places cost hides

ChargeHow it worksHow to spot it
Exchange rate marginThe provider's rate is worse than mid-market; the difference is their revenueCompare their rate against the day's mid-market rate for the same day
Transfer feeA fixed amount per transaction, sometimes scaled by amount or speedAsk for the fee schedule; on small sends this dominates
Intermediary deductionCorrespondent banks handling the transfer take a cut as it passes throughAsk whether the full amount is guaranteed to arrive
Receiving-side chargeThe recipient's bank deducts a fee on arrivalAsk the recipient what actually landed, and compare

A fifth cost exists but is invisible in these comparisons: the time your money is in transit. A transfer that takes five working days means five days during which the money is neither with you nor usable by the recipient.

4. Why small transfers cost proportionally more

A fixed fee is the same whether you send 100 or 10,000. As a percentage of the amount, it shrinks as the amount grows:

Two direct consequences:

  • Combine transfers where a fixed fee applies. One large send beats several small ones, and the saving is largest for the smallest amounts.
  • For small amounts, prioritise a low fixed fee over the best advertised rate. At 100, a 15 fee costs more than a full percentage point of rate margin.

5. Speed has a price

Instant and same-day transfers generally cost more than transfers taking one to three business days. The provider takes settlement risk and pre-funds the payout, and charges for it.

SpeedTypical cost premiumWhen it is worth it
Instant / minuteshighestGenuine emergencies
Same daymoderate to highTime-sensitive obligations
1–3 business dayslowestEverything else

If the money is not urgent, choosing the slower option is often the cheapest saving available on the transfer. Conversely, do not pay for speed on routine transfers where timing is flexible — that premium recurs every single time.

There is no permanently best provider. Margins, promotions and corridor coverage change frequently, and loyalty is rarely rewarded in this market. Re-check every few months if you send regularly.

7. A checklist before every transfer

  • Verify recipient details exactly — account number, name spelling, and any required codes such as IBAN, SWIFT/BIC, or local clearing codes. Errors are slow and expensive to reverse, and sometimes unrecoverable.
  • Check the mid-market rate at the moment you send, so you can judge the margin you are being offered.
  • Ask for the guaranteed amount received, not just the rate.
  • Confirm whether the quote is guaranteed or indicative, and for how long it is valid.
  • Ask whether intermediary and receiving-bank charges are included or may be deducted later.
  • Check the expected arrival date, not just the speed label.
  • Keep the receipt and reference number until the money has landed and been confirmed by the recipient.

8. Security: fraud patterns to recognise

Remittance is a common vector for fraud because transfers are typically irreversible. Treat these as red flags:

  • Urgency and pressure. A stranger or someone impersonating a known contact insisting the transfer must happen now.
  • Payment to someone you have not verified through a channel you independently initiated — call back on a number you already have, not one supplied in the request.
  • Requests routed through cash pickup, especially to a third party, which is a frequent feature of scams because it is hard to trace.
  • "Unexpected" authority figures — a supposed government agency, employer or family emergency demanding immediate payment.
  • Requests to overpay and refund the difference, which exposes you when the original payment later fails.
Once a transfer has been collected, recovery is often impossible. If something feels wrong, stop and verify independently before sending. Legitimate obligations survive a short delay; scams generally do not.

9. If you send money regularly

Recurring transfers deserve periodic review, because the cumulative effect of a poor margin is large. Sending 1,000 a month at 4% instead of 1% costs roughly 360 a year — every year.

  • Re-check your provider every three to six months against at least two alternatives, using the effective cost formula.
  • Consider whether consolidating monthly sends into quarterly sends is feasible, if a fixed fee applies.
  • Check whether your provider offers a better rate or lower fee at higher tiers.
  • Watch for promotional rates that expire — the rate after the promotion ends is the one you will actually live with.

10. Myths worth discarding

  • "My bank is safest, so it is worth the extra cost." Safety comes from verifying the recipient, not from which institution moves the money.
  • "Zero fee transfers are cheapest." Compare the amount received; the fee is usually in the rate.
  • "The rate I saw this morning still applies." Quotes expire, and rates move continuously.
  • "Bigger providers are always cheaper." Frequently the opposite for smaller amounts.
  • "Once I find a good provider I never need to check again." Pricing in this market changes constantly.
