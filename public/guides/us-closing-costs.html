US Closing Costs: What You Actually Pay at Settlement

Closing costs are the charges you pay to complete a mortgage that are not the down payment and not the monthly payment. They typically run 2% to 5% of the loan amount, and they are the single most common reason buyers arrive at settlement short of cash.

1. What closing costs actually are

A mortgage has three separate pools of money. The down payment is equity. The monthly payment is principal, interest, tax and insurance. Closing costs are everything else: the fees charged by the lender, the fees charged by third parties, and the prepaid items that fund your escrow account before the first payment is due.

They are not interest, and they are not negotiable as a single line — but many individual components are.

2. How much they usually are

The range most buyers see is 2% to 5% of the loan amount. On a $320,000 loan:

RateClosing costs
2%$6,400
3%$9,600
5%$16,000
Rule of thumb: budget 3% of the loan unless you have a real Loan Estimate in hand. Then add your down payment to get the true cash needed at settlement.

3. The lender charges

  • Origination fee — often 0.5% to 1% of the loan, for processing and underwriting.
  • Discount points — optional. One point costs 1% of the loan and typically buys roughly a 0.25% rate reduction. Whether it pays off depends entirely on how long you keep the loan.
  • Underwriting, processing, application fees — flat charges that vary widely between lenders.

4. The third-party charges

  • Appraisal — required by the lender to confirm value.
  • Title search and title insurance — protects against ownership defects. Often one of the larger single items.
  • Attorney or settlement agent fee — required in some states, optional in others.
  • Credit report, flood certification, tax service — small individually.
  • Survey, pest inspection, HOA transfer — property specific.

5. Prepaid items and escrow

These are not fees — they are your own money collected in advance. They are the reason closing costs feel larger than expected:

  • Prepaid interest — from settlement date to the end of that month.
  • Property tax and homeowners insurance — several months deposited into escrow up front.
  • First year of insurance premium — sometimes required in full.

Because escrow funding depends on your settlement month and local tax calendar, two identical loans can have very different cash-to-close figures.

6. How to read the Loan Estimate

US lenders must issue a Loan Estimate within three business days of application. It groups costs into labelled sections, and only a few of them can increase at closing:

  • Cannot increase — lender origination charges and most lender fees.
  • Can increase up to 10% — most third-party services, unless you choose a provider not on the lender's list.
  • Can change freely — prepaid interest, escrow deposits, and anything tied to your settlement date.
Compare the Loan Estimate against the Closing Disclosure you receive three days before settlement. Any unexplained jump in the section that was supposed to be fixed is worth challenging before you sign.

7. Ways to reduce them

  • Shop lenders on the origination line, not the headline rate. A lender advertising a lower rate may simply be charging more in fees.
  • Ask whether discount points are worth it at your horizon. If you will move or refinance within a few years, they usually are not.
  • Check whether the seller will contribute. In many markets seller concessions toward closing costs are negotiable.
  • Time your settlement late in the month. Prepaid interest is charged per day, so closing near month end reduces it.
  • Ask about lender credits. Accepting a slightly higher rate in exchange for a credit toward costs can be rational if you expect to refinance or move soon.

8. The number that actually matters

Cash to close is the sum of your down payment, closing costs and prepaid items, minus any seller credit or earnest money already paid. That — not the loan amount — is what you must have wired and available on settlement day.

9. Cash to close: the number that actually matters

Closing costs are only one part of what you must bring to settlement. The figure on the final page of your Closing Disclosure is cash to close, and it combines four things:

ComponentOn a $400,000 home, 20% down, $320,000 loan
Down payment$80,000
Closing costs at 3%$9,600
Prepaid escrow and prepaid interestroughly $1,500 to $4,000
Seller credits and earnest moneyminus whatever was agreed
Cash to closeabout $88,000 to $92,000
Buyers who budget the down payment alone arrive roughly $11,000 short. Cash to close is the figure to plan around, never the down payment.

10. Where the money can come from

  • Seller concessions. The seller agrees to cover part of your costs. Loan programs cap how much they may contribute, commonly 3% to 9% of the price depending on the loan type and down payment.
  • Lender credits. The lender covers costs in exchange for a higher rate. Useful if you are short on cash and expect to refinance or move within a few years.
  • Agent or builder incentives. Often restricted to specific vendors, and sometimes taxable.
  • Gift funds. Permitted by most programs with a letter confirming the money is not a loan.

Each of these trades cash today against cost later. A seller concession reduces what you bring to closing; a lender credit raises what you pay every month.

11. Why costs vary so much by location

Closing costs are partly local. The differences that matter most:

  • Transfer and recordation taxes. Set by state and sometimes county; a few jurisdictions charge well above 1% of the price.
  • Attorney versus escrow closing. Some states require an attorney, adding a fee that others do not have.
  • Title insurance regulation. Some states set the rate by law; others let insurers compete.
  • Whether the seller traditionally pays certain items. Local convention shifts costs between the parties without changing the total.

This is why a national average is only a starting point. Ask a local lender or settlement agent for the figures that apply in your county.

12. Do discount points pay off?

One point costs 1% of the loan — $3,200 on $320,000 — and typically buys about a 0.25% rate reduction. On this example, 6.5% to 6.25%:

  • New payment: $1,970.30, a saving of $52.32 a month
  • Break-even: $3,200 ÷ $52.32 ≈ 61 months, about five years

Points are worth buying when you will hold the loan well past the break-even, and are a poor use of cash when you will not. If that cash would otherwise sit in the down payment and reduce mortgage insurance, compare both uses — the insurance saving often comes first.

13. What you can actually negotiate

  • Lender fees. Origination, processing and underwriting charges are the most flexible items on the estimate.
  • Title insurance and settlement fees. Often shoppable; the lender cannot require you to use a particular provider.
  • Rate versus credit. Ask for the same loan priced at a higher rate with a larger credit, then compare the break-even.
  • Not negotiable: transfer taxes, recordation charges and appraisal fees are set by third parties or by law.

Ask for a revised Loan Estimate after negotiating, and re-check the cash-to-close figure rather than the individual lines.
