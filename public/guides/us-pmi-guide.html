Private Mortgage Insurance: When You Pay It, When It Ends, and How to Avoid It

PMI is the single most misunderstood line on a US mortgage statement. It protects the lender, not you, and it can add well over a hundred dollars to every monthly payment. This guide explains exactly when it is required, how much it costs, the federal rule that forces it to end, and the four legitimate ways to remove it sooner — with the arithmetic on each.

1. What PMI actually is

Private mortgage insurance is a policy that pays the lender if you default. You pay the premium; the lender receives the benefit. It exists because a small down payment means the lender has little cushion if the property has to be sold in a foreclosure.

It applies to conventional loans. FHA loans have a different, usually more expensive version called MIP, and VA loans have none at all.

2. When it is required

The rule of thumb: a down payment below 20% on a conventional loan triggers PMI. Expressed as loan-to-value, that is an LTV above 80%. Put 20% or more down and the requirement disappears.

3. What it costs

Premiums typically run between 0.3% and 1.5% of the original loan amount per year, depending on your credit score and the LTV. It is charged monthly and usually bundled into the escrow payment.

Worked example on a $400,000 purchase with 10% down:

  • Loan amount: $360,000 (LTV 90%)
  • PMI at 0.5% annually: $360,000 × 0.5% ÷ 12 = $150.00 per month
  • Added to PITI, the total reaches $2,942.11

A stronger credit profile at the same LTV might price nearer 0.3%, cutting that to about $90 per month. The premium is not fixed — it is quoted against your risk.

4. When it ends automatically

Under the federal Homeowners Protection Act, the servicer must terminate PMI automatically once the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original property value, provided the loan is current.

On the example above — a $400,000 home with a $360,000 loan at 6.5% — the balance does not reach $312,000 until month 109, roughly 9.1 years. Over that period you would pay about $16,350 in premiums.

That figure is worth staring at. PMI is not a small closing fee; on a low-down-payment loan it is a five-figure cost.

5. Requesting cancellation at 80%

You do not have to wait for the 78% trigger. You may request cancellation once the balance reaches 80% of the original value, as long as you have a good payment history and can evidence the value. On the example, that threshold arrives meaningfully earlier than month 109 — every month you shave off is $150 saved.

Practically: if you are close to 80% and have not been billed for cancellation, ask your servicer. It is not always applied without prompting.

6. Four ways to remove or avoid it

  • Reach 20% equity faster. Extra principal payments pull the 80% date forward. Each month earlier saves one full premium.
  • Use appreciation. If the property value has risen, a new appraisal may put you under 80% LTV even though your balance has barely moved. Policies vary on seasoning requirements.
  • Piggyback a second loan. An 80/10/10 structure — first mortgage at 80%, a second at 10%, 10% down — avoids PMI entirely, at the cost of a higher rate on the second.
  • Lender-paid mortgage insurance. The lender covers the policy and builds the cost into a slightly higher interest rate. It is permanent, so it suits borrowers who expect to move or refinance soon.

7. The refinance option

If rates have fallen or your credit has improved, refinancing can clear PMI in one step: the new loan is sized at 80% LTV or less, so no insurance is required. Compare the closing costs against the monthly saving to see whether it pays back before you expect to move.

8. What PMI costs at each down payment level

The premium is quoted as an annual percentage of the loan and charged monthly. It falls as the down payment rises, and it rises sharply as the credit score falls. On a $400,000 purchase, using mid-range credit assumptions:

9. Conventional PMI versus FHA MIP

Both protect the lender, but they end very differently. This is the most consequential distinction in US mortgage insurance.

Conventional PMIFHA MIP
Upfront chargeNone1.75% of the loan, usually financed
Ends automaticallyYes, at 78% LTVOnly when LTV was 90% or less at origination
Typical durationA few years11 years, or the life of the loan
Removed by refinancingYesYes, but the new loan must be conventional

For an FHA loan above 90% LTV at origination, the annual MIP runs for the entire term. Over 30 years that is a six-figure difference against an otherwise identical conventional loan.

10. Working out the exact month your PMI ends

The automatic termination date is the month the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original value — not the month you actually get there. You can derive it in three steps:

  • Take the original property value and multiply by 78%. On a $400,000 home that is $312,000.
  • Run the amortisation schedule at your rate and term, and find the month the balance first falls to that figure. Here it is month 109.
  • Multiply the monthly premium by that month count. $150 × 109 = $16,350.

Any extra principal payment moves the date earlier. Paying an additional $200 a month on the example above pulls the 78% date forward by roughly two years, saving about $3,600 of premiums — a return that beats most guaranteed investments.

11. Borrower-paid versus lender-paid mortgage insurance

  • Borrower-paid (BPMI). You pay the monthly premium. It ends when the LTV thresholds are met, and it disappears from the payment automatically.
  • Lender-paid (LPMI). The lender pays the policy and charges you a slightly higher rate instead. There is no separate line item, but the higher rate is permanent — you keep paying it after 78% equity is reached.

LPMI suits borrowers who expect to move or refinance within a few years. For anyone holding the loan long term, BPMI is almost always cheaper, because it ends and LPMI does not.

12. Is mortgage insurance tax deductible?

The tax treatment of mortgage insurance premiums has changed repeatedly at the federal level, is subject to income phase-outs, and has at times expired entirely. Treat any deduction as a possibility to confirm with a tax professional rather than as a planning assumption — build your budget on the gross cost.

13. What to ask your servicer

  • What is my current LTV, and on what value is it based — the original price or a new appraisal?
  • Am I eligible for cancellation at 80% now, and what evidence do you require?
  • When is automatic termination scheduled, under the original amortisation schedule?
  • If I make a lump-sum principal payment, will you re-amortise the loan or keep the payment and shorten the term?
  • Is my policy BPMI or LPMI? If LPMI, can it be removed at all?

Servicers do not volunteer cancellations. Every one of these questions has produced a real refund for someone.
