When Refinancing Makes Sense: The Break-Even Calculation

Refinancing is not complicated, but it is frequently done badly. The decision comes down to one number: how many months of savings it takes to repay the closing costs. This guide walks through the break-even formula, shows what happens when you shorten the term instead, and lists the cases where refinancing is clearly the wrong move.

1. The only formula you need

Refinance arithmetic reduces to a single division:

Break-even in months = closing costs ÷ monthly saving

Every other consideration — rate, term, lender — feeds into that one figure. If you move before hitting break-even, you paid for a benefit you never collected.

2. A rate-and-term refi, with numbers

Current loan: $300,000 at 7% with 30 years, payment $1,995.91.

Refinance at 6% over a fresh 30 years:

  • New payment: $1,798.65
  • Monthly saving: $197.26
  • Total interest saved across the full term: $71,012
  • Closing costs: $6,000

Break-even: $6,000 ÷ $197.26 = 30.4 months, roughly two and a half years.

Plan to stay longer than that and the refi is clearly worthwhile. Expect to move within a year and you would be paying $6,000 to save about $2,367.

3. Why restarting the clock can cost you

There is a trap in the example above. After several years of payments, the original 30-year loan had only, say, 25 years left. Refinancing into a new 30-year term stretches it back out. The payment drops partly because the rate fell — and partly because you re-extended the schedule, which raises total interest.

Compare against refinancing into the remaining term instead of a fresh 30 years. Lower payment is not automatically lower cost; check total interest, not just the monthly figure.

4. Shortening the term instead

Same $300,000 at 6%, but over 20 years rather than 30:

  • Payment: $2,149.29 — about $153 more per month than before
  • Total interest: $215,830 across 240 payments ($515,830 total repaid)

The monthly number goes up, yet this is often the better financial move. Shorten the term when the rate improvement is modest but you want the loan gone sooner.

5. Cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refi replaces the old loan with a larger one and hands you the difference. It converts home equity into cash, usually at a lower rate than a personal loan or credit card. The risk is structural: your balance goes up and your equity goes down. Treat it as borrowing, not as saving.

6. Watch for these

  • Prepayment penalties — check whether your current loan charges one.
  • Section 32 / high-cost loans — special federal protections apply; understand them before signing.
  • Escrow restart — you may need cash at closing to seed a new escrow account.
  • Discount points — buying the rate down only pays off if you stay past the points break-even too.
  • No-closing-cost offers — usually a higher rate rather than a genuine discount. Compare total interest.

7. When not to refinance

  • The rate improvement is small relative to the costs.
  • You are likely to move before break-even.
  • You are late in the term, where most of the payment is already principal — restarting wastes that progress.
  • Your income is unstable and the payment reduction is the only thing making the budget work.

Run the break-even months, then ask honestly how much longer you expect to keep the loan. That comparison is the decision.

8. How much of a rate drop is worth acting on

The conventional rule of thumb is that a reduction of roughly 0.5 to 1 percentage point justifies a refinance, but the rule hides the real variable: how long you will keep the new loan. On a $320,000 balance:

Rate improvementMonthly savingSaving over 10 years
0.25% (7.00% → 6.75%)$52.90$6,348
0.50% (7.00% → 6.50%)$106.35$12,762

Against $6,000 of closing costs, the 0.25% improvement takes about 113 months to break even — nearly a decade. The 0.50% improvement takes about 56 months. The smaller drop is only worth it if you are certain to hold the loan far longer, or if the lender is covering the costs.

9. Shortening the term: the numbers behind the higher payment

Refinancing $300,000 from 7% over 30 years into 6% over 20 years raises the payment from $1,995.91 to $2,149.29 — about $153 more each month. The interest picture is where it wins:

  • Interest over the new 20-year term: $215,830
  • Interest if you refinanced into a fresh 30 years at 6%: $347,515
  • Difference: $131,685

The monthly payment goes up, but the total cost falls by more than six figures. This is why "the payment went up" is not the same as "the refinance was a bad idea" — always compare total interest, not just the monthly figure.

10. No-closing-cost refinancing

A no-closing-cost refinance does not mean the costs disappear. It means they are paid one of two ways:

  • A higher rate. The lender credits the costs in exchange for pricing the loan above the market rate. You pay nothing today and slightly more every month, forever.
  • Rolling them into the balance. You finance the costs, which means paying interest on them for the life of the loan.

Compare the same loan with and without the credit. Divide the rate difference into the costs saved: if the credit is $3,000 and costs you 0.125% more, the break-even is roughly the same calculation as any other refinance.

11. Refinancing out of FHA MIP

If your FHA loan started above 90% LTV, the annual MIP runs for the life of the loan. Once you have 20% equity, refinancing into a conventional loan removes it entirely. This is often the strongest refinance case that exists, because the saving is not a small rate difference — it is the elimination of a permanent line item.

On the earlier example, $180.01 a month removed is equivalent to a rate reduction of well over half a percentage point, and the benefit continues for the remaining term.

12. Rate locks and timing

  • Lock length must cover the closing. A 30-day lock on a 45-day close leaves you exposed to a re-price.
  • Float-down options cost something. Some lenders allow one reduction if rates fall before closing, usually for a fee or a slightly higher starting rate.
  • A lock is not a quote. It applies to a specific loan amount, term and program. Changing any of them can invalidate it.

13. A refinance checklist

  • Get a Loan Estimate from at least two lenders, and compare the same day — quotes move daily.
  • Separate the rate from the costs. The lowest rate is rarely the cheapest loan.
  • Confirm whether the new term restarts at 30 years or matches your remaining term, and compare total interest under both.
  • Check for a prepayment penalty on the existing loan.
  • Confirm whether you must keep the loan for a minimum period, or repay the credit if you refinance again.
  • Compute the break-even in months and compare it honestly against how long you expect to stay.

Every refinance that goes wrong does so for the same reason: the monthly payment improved and nobody checked the total.
