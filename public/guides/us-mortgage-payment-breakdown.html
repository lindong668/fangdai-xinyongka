How a US Mortgage Payment Is Built: PITI, Principal and the True Monthly Cost

The number a lender quotes you is rarely the number you actually pay each month. A US mortgage payment has up to six separate components, and three of them are invisible in the headline rate. This guide breaks down every layer with real numbers, shows why two borrowers with the same rate can pay hundreds of dollars apart, and explains how to reconstruct your own payment before you sign.

1. The loan is not the price

Start with the one distinction that trips up nearly every first-time buyer: the mortgage is based on the amount you borrow, not on what the house costs.

  • Home price: $400,000
  • Down payment at 20%: $80,000
  • Loan amount (principal): $320,000

Every payment figure below is calculated on the $320,000, never on the $400,000. Getting this backwards is the most common reason online estimates feel wrong.

2. Principal and interest: the part everyone quotes

At 6.5% over 30 years, the standard amortising payment on $320,000 works out to $2,022.62 per month. That single number is what most calculators show you, and it is only part of the story.

Total paid over 360 months: $728,142. Total interest: $408,142 — meaning you repay roughly $1.28 for every $1 borrowed. Extending to 30 years rather than 15 is what makes that ratio so high.

3. The PITI formula

US lenders underwrite against a four-letter bundle called PITI. Learn it once and you can sanity-check any quote:

  • P — Principal, the slice of the balance you pay down
  • I — Interest, the cost of borrowing what is left
  • T — Property tax, collected monthly into an escrow account
  • I — Homeowners insurance, also escrowed

On the same $400,000 home, at a 1.2% annual property tax rate and a 0.35% insurance rate:

  • Property tax: $400,000 × 1.2% ÷ 12 = $400.00 per month
  • Insurance: $400,000 × 0.35% ÷ 12 = $116.67 per month
  • Full PITI: $2,022.62 + $400.00 + $116.67 = $2,539.28

The rate-quoted figure was $2,022.62. The money actually leaving your account is $2,539.28 — a gap of about $517 every month.

4. The three layers that hide on top

PITI still is not the whole payment. Depending on the loan and the property, three more items can appear:

  • Mortgage insurance — required on conventional loans when the down payment is under 20%, and on all FHA loans. This is a real monthly cost, not a fee.
  • HOA dues — condos and planned communities charge these; they can run from under $100 to well over $500 monthly and are usually not escrowed.
  • Flood or earthquake insurance — separate from homeowners insurance, and mandatory in designated zones.

5. Why the same rate costs different amounts

Take the identical house and the identical 6.5% rate, but change the down payment from 20% to 10%:

  • Loan rises to $360,000, so principal and interest becomes $2,275.44
  • Private mortgage insurance at 0.5% annually adds $150.00
  • Total monthly: $2,942.11

That is $402.83 more per month — about $4,834 a year — for putting $40,000 less down. The rate never changed; the loan structure did. This is why comparing lenders on rate alone can be misleading.

6. Escrow: why your payment moves after closing

Most US loans collect taxes and insurance through an escrow account. The lender estimates the annual bill, divides by twelve, and holds the money. Every year they re-run the analysis: if your property assessment rose, your monthly payment goes up even though your interest rate is fixed. A fixed-rate mortgage fixes P and I only — T and I in the acronym float for the life of the loan.

7. How to reconstruct your own number

To sanity-check any quote, work through it in this order:

  • Price minus down payment = the principal you are financing
  • Apply the rate over the term to get principal and interest
  • Add one-twelfth of the annual property tax — look up your county rate, do not assume
  • Add one-twelfth of the annual insurance premium
  • Add mortgage insurance if the down payment is under 20%
  • Add HOA dues if the property has them

Run the full stack, not just the interest rate. The gap between the headline number and the real one is where budgets break.

8. How much the payment moves when escrow changes

The principal and interest part of a fixed-rate payment never changes. Everything else does. That is why the payment most buyers budget for is not the payment they make in year three.

Using the same $400,000 home, a 1.2% property tax rate and a 0.35% insurance rate:

A $51.67 monthly increase is $620 a year that no rate lock protects you from. Escrow changes are the single most common reason a "fixed" mortgage payment goes up.

Property tax reassessment is the bigger risk. In many states the assessed value is reset when the property changes hands, so the tax figure on your Loan Estimate is based on the seller's assessment, not on what you paid. Budget for the higher number.

9. The same rate, four very different payments

Two borrowers can both hold a 6.5% 30-year loan and pay hundreds of dollars apart, because the rate only governs the loan — not the tax, the insurance or the mortgage insurance. On the same $400,000 home:

Dropping from 20% down to 5% down raises the monthly payment by $616.75 while making the loan $60,000 larger. Half of that increase is mortgage insurance, which buys the lender protection — not you.

10. Escrow shortages: the year-two surprise

Your servicer collects a fixed amount each month for tax and insurance, then pays the actual bills when they arrive. If the real bill exceeds what was collected, you have a shortage, and the servicer recovers it by raising next year's monthly payment while also collecting the shortfall.

  • A $600 annual shortfall adds $50 a month to the next year's payment on top of the higher amount needed for the year ahead.
  • Most servicers allow you to pay the shortage in one lump sum instead, which keeps the monthly payment lower.
  • Federal rules cap how large a surplus the escrow account may hold before it must be refunded, so an over-collection comes back to you as a credit.

This is mechanical, not a penalty — but it is why the payment you agreed to at closing is rarely the payment you make twelve months later.

11. What lenders count as "your housing payment"

When a lender decides whether you qualify, the housing figure is not your PITI alone. It is PITI plus any mandatory association dues, and it excludes everything discretionary.

  • Counted: principal, interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA or condo association dues, and any ground rent.
  • Not counted: utilities, internet, maintenance, repairs, and any insurance you buy voluntarily such as flood coverage beyond what is required.

On a condo with $350 monthly dues, a borrower who qualifies on a $2,539.29 PITI is in fact qualifying on $2,889.29. That difference alone can move the approval amount by tens of thousands of dollars.

12. Three checks on any quote you receive

  • Separate the loan from the price. Confirm the payment is computed on the amount borrowed, not on the purchase price. A quote built on $400,000 instead of $320,000 overstates the payment by roughly $505.
  • Ask whether tax and insurance are included. A "payment" that omits escrow is not comparable to one that includes it.
  • Ask what the escrow assumption is. The tax rate and insurance premium used in the estimate matter as much as the rate itself.

These three questions take two minutes and catch the majority of quoting errors.

13. The mistakes that make estimates wrong

  • Using the house price as the loan. The most common error by a wide margin.
  • Ignoring mortgage insurance. Below 20% down it is a permanent line item until cancellation, not a one-off fee.
  • Assuming the payment is flat. Only the P&I portion is fixed. Budget for escrow movement every year.
  • Forgetting HOA dues. On condos and planned communities this is often the largest single non-mortgage housing cost.
  • Comparing quotes with different assumptions. Two lenders using different tax or insurance figures are not quoting the same thing.

Reconstruct the payment yourself once, from the loan amount upward, and every quote you receive afterwards becomes easy to judge.
