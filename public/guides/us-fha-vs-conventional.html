FHA vs Conventional vs VA Loans: How the Three Main US Mortgage Types Compare

Choosing between an FHA loan, a conventional loan and a VA loan changes your down payment, your monthly cost and how long you carry insurance. The right answer depends almost entirely on your down payment and your credit profile. This guide puts all three side by side with the same house and the same rate, so the real monthly difference is visible.

1. Conventional loans

A conventional loan is any mortgage not insured by a government agency. It is the default for borrowers with solid credit and a meaningful down payment.

  • Down payment: as low as 3% for some first-time-buyer programmes, though 20% is the benchmark
  • Mortgage insurance: PMI required below 20% down, and it can be cancelled once you reach 80% LTV
  • Best for: borrowers who can put 20% down, or who expect to cross 80% equity within a few years

The decisive advantage is that conventional PMI is temporary. It ends.

2. FHA loans

FHA loans are insured by the Federal Housing Administration and are designed for buyers with smaller down payments or thinner credit. The trade-off is mortgage insurance that is harder to escape.

  • Down payment: as low as 3.5%
  • Upfront MIP: 1.75% of the loan, usually financed into the balance
  • Annual MIP: charged monthly; on higher-LTV loans it runs for the life of the loan
  • Best for: buyers whose credit score or cash savings rule out a conventional loan

3. The FHA cost, with numbers

Same $400,000 house, same 6.5% rate, but only 3.5% down instead of 20%:

  • Down payment: $14,000 instead of $80,000 — a cash saving of $66,000
  • Base loan: $386,000
  • Upfront MIP at 1.75%: $6,755, financed into the loan, so the balance becomes $392,755
  • Principal and interest: $2,482.48
  • Annual MIP at 0.55%: $180.01 per month
  • Total monthly with tax and insurance: $3,179.16

Compare that with $2,539.28 for a 20%-down conventional loan on the same house. The FHA borrower pays $639.87 more every month — about $7,678 a year — in exchange for keeping $66,000 in the bank at closing.

Whether that is a good trade depends on what the $66,000 does. Earning nothing in a low-yield account, the insurance cost likely outweighs it. Used to avoid a far worse borrowing cost elsewhere, it can be rational.

4. VA loans

For eligible service members, veterans and some surviving spouses, VA loans are the strongest product on the market.

  • Down payment: frequently zero
  • Mortgage insurance: none, monthly or upfront
  • Funding fee: a one-time charge that varies by usage and down payment, and can be financed
  • Best for: anyone who qualifies

The absence of monthly insurance is the whole argument. Against the FHA example above, it removes both the $180.01 annual MIP and the financed upfront premium.

5. USDA loans

Worth knowing if you are buying in a qualifying rural or suburban area: USDA loans offer zero down payment with no monthly mortgage insurance, only a guarantee fee and a modest annual charge. Income limits and property location both apply.

6. How to choose

  • Can you put 20% down? Take the conventional loan. No PMI, and the lowest long-run cost.
  • Below 20% but credit is strong? Conventional with PMI usually beats FHA, because that PMI disappears at 80% LTV while FHA MIP on a high-LTV loan does not.
  • Credit score below the conventional threshold? FHA is likely the route, and refinancing to conventional later is a legitimate plan.
  • VA eligible? Start there, and compare against conventional before deciding.

Model each option against your own down payment and credit profile. The ranking can flip entirely depending on those two inputs.

7. How long FHA mortgage insurance actually lasts

This is where FHA loans are most often misjudged. The duration depends on the LTV at origination, not on how fast you pay the loan down:

LTV at originationAnnual MIP duration
90% or less11 years
Above 90%The life of the loan

With 3.5% down, the LTV starts above 90%, so the annual MIP runs for all 30 years unless you refinance into a conventional loan. On the worked example that is $180.01 a month for three hundred and sixty months — about $64,800 in premiums.

The practical consequence: an FHA loan with a small down payment is usually a loan you refinance out of, not a loan you hold to term. Factor the eventual refinance cost into the comparison.

8. FHA versus a 3%-down conventional loan

The fair comparison for an FHA borrower with 3.5% down is a conventional loan with 3% down, not one with 20% down. On the same $400,000 house at 6.5%:

FHA, 3.5% downConventional, 3% down
Base loan$386,000$388,000
Upfront charge financed$6,755$0
Principal and interest$2,482.48$2,452.42
Monthly insurance$180.01$242.50
Full monthly payment$3,179.16$3,211.59

The FHA loan is $32.43 a month cheaper here, and its insurance is scheduled to end after 11 years only if the starting LTV was 90% or below — which it is not. The conventional loan's PMI, by contrast, ends at 78% LTV regardless. Run both before choosing.

9. What the VA funding fee costs

VA loans carry no monthly mortgage insurance, but most borrowers pay a one-off funding fee that can be financed into the loan. On a $400,000 zero-down loan:

  • First use, no down payment: 2.3% — $9,200
  • Subsequent use, no down payment: 3.3% — $13,200
  • With 5% or more down: the fee drops to 1.5% or lower
  • Exempt: veterans receiving VA disability compensation, and some surviving spouses

Even financed, a one-off 2.3% beats a monthly premium that runs for years. On the example, the VA payment including tax and insurance is $3,044.94 against $3,179.16 for FHA — and the VA borrower put no money down at all.

10. USDA loans: the rural and suburban option

USDA guaranteed loans offer zero down payment for eligible properties and income limits. The cost structure is:

  • Guarantee fee: 1% of the loan upfront, usually financed
  • Annual fee: 0.35% of the declining balance, charged monthly, for the life of the loan

The annual fee is lower than FHA MIP, but it never ends. USDA works best where the property qualifies and the buyer genuinely has no down payment; otherwise the geography restriction usually outweighs the benefit.

11. Jumbo loans: above the conforming limit

Loans above the conforming limit in your county are non-conforming, or jumbo. They are not insured by any agency, and lenders price them on their own terms: typically a slightly higher rate, a larger down payment, and stricter reserve and credit requirements. Mortgage insurance is not a feature — the lender simply holds the risk.
