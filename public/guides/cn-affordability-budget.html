China Home Budget: From Monthly Payment to Purchase Price

The question is never "how much can I borrow" but "how much should I borrow". Lenders answer the first; only you can answer the second. This guide turns a monthly budget into a loan size, and then into a realistic purchase budget.

1. Start from the payment, not the price

Most buyers begin with a property price and work backwards, which inverts the decision. The durable constraint is the monthly payment you can sustain for years, including the months when something goes wrong.

2. How much payment is reasonable

A widely used guideline is that housing should not consume more than about 30% to 40% of household monthly income. On common income levels:

Monthly household incomeAt 30%At 40%At 50%
¥10,000¥3,000¥4,000¥5,000
¥15,000¥4,500¥6,000¥7,500
¥20,000¥6,000¥8,000¥10,000
Above roughly 50%, a single income shock becomes a default risk. The limit a lender will approve and the limit you should accept are different numbers.

3. Turning a payment into a loan size

At 3.5% over 30 years, each level of monthly payment supports a specific loan:

Monthly paymentSupported loan
¥3,000≈ ¥668,000
¥4,500≈ ¥1,002,000
¥6,000≈ ¥1,336,000

Notice how little a small payment increase buys. Raising the payment by ¥1,500 adds only about ¥334,000 of borrowing — often less than the down payment difference between two properties.

4. From loan to purchase budget

The loan is only part of the purchase. Working forwards from your cash:

  1. Total cash available — savings plus any family support.
  2. Reserve that must not be touched — commonly six to twelve months of expenses.
  3. What remains is the true down payment.
  4. Add taxes and fees — deed tax, and on second-hand purchases, VAT and income tax that may be negotiated onto the buyer.
  5. What remains after that is what actually goes to the purchase price.
Purchase budget = usable down payment + supported loan − taxes and fees. Skipping step 5 is the most common reason buyers find themselves short at settlement.

5. The costs that sit outside the loan

  • Deed tax — on a first home of 140㎡ or less, 1% under the policy in force since December 2024.
  • VAT on second-hand purchases — exempt if the seller has held two years or more; otherwise charged on the full price.
  • Income tax — waived when the property is the seller's only home held five years or more.
  • Agency commission, maintenance fund, registration charges.

6. Stress-testing the payment

Before committing, check the payment against three scenarios:

  • Income falls by 20% for six months — can you still pay?
  • Rates rise — if your loan re-prices with the LPR, model the payment with the reference one percentage point higher.
  • A major expense arrives — medical, education, or a dependent.

7. Holding period matters more than rate

The total cost of a mortgage is dominated by how long you keep it, not by small differences in rate. A buyer who repays early or sells in five years is barely affected by a 0.25% rate difference, while a buyer who holds for thirty years is affected substantially. Decide your likely holding period before optimising for rate.

8. A simple sequence

  1. Fix the sustainable monthly payment from your own budget.
  2. Convert it into a supported loan at your actual rate and term.
  3. Work out usable cash after reserves, taxes and fees.
  4. Add the two to get your real purchase budget.
  5. Stress-test, then view properties inside that number only.

9. Turning a payment into a loan size

At 3.5% over 30 years, each level of monthly payment supports a specific loan. This is the step most buyers skip, and it is the one that makes the budget real:

Monthly paymentSupported loan
¥3,000about ¥668,085
¥4,500about ¥1,002,127
¥6,000about ¥1,336,170
Raising the payment by ¥1,500 adds only about ¥334,000 of borrowing. Small increases in what you can pay each month buy surprisingly little extra house.

Notice what this implies: if two properties differ by more than ¥334,000 in price, no realistic increase in monthly payment bridges the gap on its own. The difference has to come from the down payment.

10. From loan size to purchase price

The loan is only part of the purchase. Working forwards:

  • Total cash available — savings plus any family support.
  • Reserve that must not be touched — commonly six to twelve months of expenses.
  • What remains is the true down payment.
  • Subtract taxes and fees — deed tax, and on second-hand purchases any VAT or income tax negotiated onto the buyer.
  • Purchase budget = usable down payment + supported loan − taxes and fees.

Skipping the tax line is the most common reason buyers find themselves short at settlement. On a ¥3,000,000 first home of 95㎡ the deed tax alone is ¥30,000.

11. What sits outside the loan

  • Deed tax. 1% for a first home of 140㎡ or less under the policy in force since December 2024.
  • VAT on second-hand purchases. Exempt if the seller has held two years or more; otherwise charged on the full price at a 3% levy rate.
  • Income tax. Waived when the property is the seller's only home held five years or more.
  • Agency commission, maintenance fund, registration and survey charges. Commonly 1% to 3% of the price in total.

12. The 2026 purchase-loan interest subsidy

A central fiscal interest subsidy on residential mortgages took effect nationally on 1 October 2026, with an initial term of one year. Where all of the following apply, the finance authorities subsidise one percentage point a year on the loan principal:

  • The loan is a newly issued commercial personal housing loan for a first home — not a replacement of an existing loan.
  • The dwelling has a floor area of 120㎡ or less.
  • The purchase price is ¥1,500,000 or less.
  • The subsidised principal is capped at ¥1,000,000 per household, for a maximum of five years.

On a full ¥1,000,000 of subsidised principal held for five years, one percentage point a year is up to ¥50,000 — though the actual amount is lower because the principal declines as you repay. The subsidy is handled by the bank and applied automatically at each repayment, and it does not stack with the preferential provident fund rate or with support for affordable housing.

Because the subsidy is temporary and subject to conditions, treat it as a reduction in cost where it applies rather than as a reason to borrow more.

13. Stress-testing the payment

Before committing, check the payment against three scenarios:

  • Income falls by 20% for six months. Can you still pay without drawing on the reserve?
  • Rates rise. If your loan reprices with the LPR, model the payment with the reference one percentage point higher. On ¥1,000,000 over 30 years, moving from 3.5% to 4.5% raises the payment by roughly ¥560 a month.
  • A major expense arrives. Medical, education, or a dependent.

A payment that only works in the base case is not a budget — it is a forecast.

14. A sequence that works

  • Decide the monthly payment you can sustain in a bad year, not a good one.
  • Convert it into a loan size at the current rate.
  • Work out your true down payment after reserving six to twelve months of expenses.
  • Subtract taxes and fees from the cash, not from the loan.
  • Stress-test the resulting payment against the three scenarios above.
  • Then, and only then, start looking at properties.

Doing it in this order turns the property search from an emotional exercise into an arithmetic one — and it is the single most reliable way to avoid buying above what you can actually carry.
